Historical version · Practical version · What they used → what you're using

How to read this. Every recipe has two halves. The HISTORICAL block is what the archaeology, the period text or the unbroken tradition actually supports — including where it supports nothing and I'm inferring. The YOUR KITCHEN block is a bakeable formula in your stock, at weights that work in a domestic oven.

The tan swap bar under each pair spells out the conversion: original grain on the left, your substitute on the right, and one line on why it holds or where it breaks. Some breaks are real — barley and buckwheat have no honest substitute in your pantry, and I say so rather than fudging it.

One big thing in your favour: khorasan is not merely "like" emmer. Triticum turgidum ssp. turanicum and T. turgidum ssp. dicoccum are two subspecies of the same tetraploid species, so the grain chemistry is genuinely close and most of the ancient breads here are emmer breads. The dough is not identical, though — khorasan is higher-protein and stronger than emmer, and free-threshing where emmer is hulled. Same species predicts the genome, not the handling.

The conversion table

They usedYou useHow close, and what shifts
Emmer
T. turgidum ssp. dicoccum		Khorasan, wholemeal
freshly milled from your berries		The best swap here. Same species, sibling subspecies — both tetraploid AABB, both nutty and sweet. But manage two differences: khorasan is free-threshing (no parching and pounding), and it is higher-protein and stronger than emmer, sitting nearer durum in handling. Hydration: Samuel measured hand-ground emmer meal at 82% and Bats' tested Egyptian formula runs at 75%; start khorasan at 70% and add. Much of that gap is milling coarseness, not the grain.
Wild / domesticated einkorn
T. monococcum		Einkorn, wholemeal
your berries, unsifted		Exact. The one grain here you can match outright. Keep the bran in — every einkorn find in the record is wholemeal.
Barley
Hordeum vulgare, incl. bere		No honest substitute. Buy hulled barley or beremeal.Barley is defined by what it lacks: no functional gluten at all, so it makes paste and flatcake, never a loaf. Rye is the nearest thing you own but rye's stickiness is pentosan-driven, a completely different mouthfeel. Hulled barley berries are cheap and mill fine — worth getting, since four recipes here are barley-led.
Free-threshing wheat
Roman siligo, medieval wastel		T65 Blanche, or T80 BiseVery good. Siligo was milled and sieved repeatedly — that's an extraction ladder, exactly what the T numbers encode. T65 ≈ the fine sieved grades (siligo, manchet, wastel); T80 ≈ the everyday grade; T110 ≈ tourte / cheat bread.
Rye
Secale cereale		Rye, from your berries
T110–T170 for the dark grades		Exact. For rugbrød and pumpernickel you want the berries whole or cracked, not just milled — those breads are built on soaked kernels.
Club-rush tuber
Bolboschoenus glaucus		Tigernut (chufa) flour
or water-chestnut flour		Better than it sounds. Tigernut is Cyperus esculentus — same family (Cyperaceae), same wetland sedge tuber, same cell walls that stay fibrous after cooking. That fibrousness is precisely why the excavators described the original as "gritty… but a bit sweet as well."
Oats
Avena		Buy oatmeal — medium and pinheadCheap, and the grade matters more than the source. Pinhead gives the coarse Highland oatcake; medium gives the thin brittle one.
SpeltKhorasan (nutty route) or T80 (structure route)Spelt is hexaploid and holds more structure than khorasan. If a recipe uses spelt for flavour, go khorasan; if it uses spelt to build the loaf, go T80.
Durum semolaKhorasan, milled coarse and re-siftedGood. Durum is T. turgidum ssp. durum — again the same species as khorasan. Sift out the bran and you have a workable semola stand-in.
Buckwheat, teff, milletBuy them.No wheat substitutes. All three are non-cereals or non-wheat cereals whose whole point is what they aren't.
Levain / barm / zymeLev'bléUse it as it is. No recipe here asks you to build a stiff levain. The Roman and medieval doughs run at 52–58% hydration and a wet starter fights that, so the formulas use a small dose instead — about 4.6% of the flour, where the stiff levains they replace ran 10–25%. In prefermented flour that is roughly 2% against 7–17%, so the water the starter carries is a rounding error. You pay in time rather than hydration — bulk runs roughly twice as long as the same dough on a stiff levain, and the times in the recipes below are written for this dose, at 24 °C. If you would rather run a live culture, the levain starter card raises one from rye and water and carries the formula for a 750 g loaf built on it.
The einkorn rule, since it runs through everything here. One HMW-glutenin locus, no Glu-D1, and gliadin running up to 12× glutenin. Farinograph work on landrace einkorn puts stability under 1.5 minutes against 4+ for common wheat — though note that bred elite lines (ID331, Monlis) test far better and can even beat a bread-wheat control on loaf volume, so this is a property of the einkorn you're likely to have, not of the species. Practically: mix briefly, don't develop it, don't give it a long final proof, keep hydration 5–10 points under what you'd use for T65, and use Lev'blé rather than baker's yeast wherever you have the choice — fresh sourdough measurably softens einkorn crumb while doing little for common wheat. Einkorn is the one grain here where your leaven is doing structural work, not just flavour.

A few things that turned out not to be bread

Worth knowing, because recipes for all three circulate:

  • Stillfried "ring breads" (Lower Austria, ~960–900 BC). Three annular objects about 3 cm across, barley and a glume wheat. 66–70% micropores and non-gelatinised starch: they were shaped from wet cereal and air-dried without ever being baked, and they imitate the clay loom-weight rings found in the same pit. Any recipe that bakes them is inventing.
  • Tollund Man's "last meal." Reconstructed as 335 g barley, 29 g pale persicaria seed, 16 g flax, plus fish, cooked as porridge in a clay pot — with threshing waste, sand and charcoal swept up off the floor. Porridge, not bread. The researchers never cooked a replica.
  • The Ipweg "galette" (Germany, c. 713 BC). A 15 cm oval that is primarily beeswax with traces of spelt, barley and millet — a substitute bread made as an offering. Some breads were never food.
