Levain Starter Card

Raising a wild starter from rye and water, keeping it alive on a small ration, and the numbers for a 750 g loaf that never touches the mother jar.

The loaf · The build · From scratch · Where it gets adjusted

Why this page exists. Every formula over on Ancient Breads leans on Lev'blé, which is deactivated sourdough plus selected baker's yeast — flavour without the biology. This is the other route: a live culture you raise yourself, on the same grain bins, with the numbers worked out for a modern hearth loaf.

The one rule that makes it sustainable: the jar in the fridge never goes in the bread. It is seed stock. You spend a spoonful of it, grow that spoonful into what the loaf needs, and skim next week's mother off the top before the rest hits the mixing bowl.

The loaf

750 g baked, one levain build

A hearth loaf loses about 13% of its weight to steam in the oven, so a 750 g finished loaf starts as roughly 860 g of dough. Drag the sliders to re-scale — percentages are on total flour, counting the flour inside the levain.

Ingredient Weight Baker’s %
Flour — your Bise T80 / Blanche T65 blend, or bread flour 435 g 90%
Water — 80 °F / 27 °C 315 g 65%
Levain, ripe — 100% hydration, at peak 100 g 21%
Fine sea salt 10 g 2.1%
Total dough 860 g 75% hyd.

Total flour 485 g · dough 860 g · baked ≈ 750 g · build 125 g of levain

Bulk 4–6 h at 74–78 °F with four sets of folds in the first two hours, shape, then either 1–2 h at room temperature or overnight in the fridge. Read the dough, not the clock — a levain keeps its own schedule.

The build

Spending 10 g to get 100 g

The trick to never running out is that the jar in the fridge is not what goes in the bread. It is seed stock. Each bake you spend a spoonful of it, grow that spoonful into what the loaf needs, and skim the new mother off the top before the rest goes into the dough.

Night before — build

10 g mother + 57 g flour + 57 g water = 124 g. Clean jar, room temperature, loose lid. Ready in 8–12 h at 70 °F, faster if warmer.

At peak — skim the mother first

Domed, webbed with bubbles, just before it sinks back. Take 20 g off into the clean fridge jar. That is next week’s mother, and it never had to survive the mixing bowl.

Then — mix

The remaining ~100 g goes into the dough. A few grams cling to the jar; that is what the extra 4 g of build is for.

Keeping the mother between bakes

A 20 g mother in the fridge holds two to three weeks without attention. If you bake weekly you barely need to feed it at all — each build refreshes it. After a long sleep, give it two room-temperature feeds at 1:2:2 before you trust it to raise a loaf. Grey liquid on top is hooch, not death: pour it off and feed.

From scratch

Skipping the dead zone

Start on whole rye. It carries the most wild yeast and the most enzyme activity of anything in your grain bins, and freshly milled from your own berries it is about as fast a start as exists. Keep the jar at 75–80 °F — temperature, not technique, is what usually stalls a new starter.

Debra Wink’s pineapple juice method drops the pH to about 4.0 on day one. That single change suppresses Leuconostoc mesenteroides, the bacterium responsible for the day-two false rise and the day-three stall, so the yeast comes up on day 3–4 instead of day 7.

Day 1
15 g whole rye + 30 g pineapple juice
Unsweetened, 100% juice — no added sugar, no sodium benzoate. It will look like a thin batter, which is correct. Loose lid, warm spot, no discard.
Day 2
add 15 g rye + 30 g juice · no discard
The dramatic false rise does not arrive. That is the method working, not failing — there is genuinely nothing to see here.
Day 3
add 15 g rye + 30 g juice · no discard
About 135 g in the jar now. In a warm kitchen the first real yeast activity often shows up today: fine even bubbles, a smell turning from fruity to yeasty.
Day 4
keep 55 g · + 30 g flour + 30 g water
juice stops Switch to unchlorinated water from here. The culture makes its own acid now, and more juice past this point holds the yeast back rather than helping it.
Day 5–6
daily · keep 55 g · + 30 g flour + 30 g water
Once you see a clear rise and fall between feeds, go to twice daily and start cutting white flour into the rye.
Day 6–8
1:2:2 twice daily · doubles in 4–8 h
bake Two consecutive predictable doublings and a smell like yogurt and green apple. Typically two to four days ahead of the plain-water route, almost all of it saved on the stall.
Day 1
50 g whole rye + 50 g water @ 80 °F
Mix to a thick paste, scrape down the sides, cover loosely. Mark the level with a rubber band. Nothing will happen and that is correct.
Day 2
keep 50 g · + 50 g rye + 50 g water
You may get a dramatic rise and a smell like gym socks or parmesan. That is leuconostoc, not yeast, and it is about to die off. Feed anyway.
Day 3
keep 50 g · + 50 g rye + 50 g water
The dead zone. Flat, quiet, maybe a whiff of acetone. Almost everyone throws their starter out here. Don’t.
Day 4
keep 50 g · + 25 g rye + 25 g white + 50 g water
Start cutting in white flour. Small bubbles return and the smell turns sour rather than foul.
Day 5–6
twice daily · keep 30 g · + 30 g flour + 30 g water
Morning and evening now. Rise time between feeds should shorten noticeably from one day to the next — that shortening is the signal, more than the height.
Day 7+
1:2:2 · ready when it doubles in 4–8 h
bake Two consecutive predictable doublings and a smell like yogurt and green apple mean it will raise bread. Seven days is the optimistic case; ten to fourteen is common, and a cold kitchen doubles everything.

Reading it

Ignore the float test until the starter is established — it fails on perfectly good stiff or rye-heavy levains. The rubber band is the better instrument: mark the level at every feed and watch how long the doubling takes. Use it at peak or just past, never on the way down.

Reminders

Put the schedule on your phone

Feeding a starter is not hard, it is just easy to forget on day four. These build a calendar file in your browser — nothing is uploaded and nothing is stored — which you open once on your phone to drop the feeds in with an alarm on each. Times are floating, so they follow whatever timezone you are standing in.

The raise

Every feed from day one to the first bake, each carrying that day’s actual instruction, plus a weekly nudge for the mother once it is established. Follows whichever schedule is selected above.

A bake day

The build the night before at 9:30 pm, peak and skim at 8:00 am, then mix, folds, shape, preheat and bake — timed to come out of the oven around 4 pm.

On a phone

The file lands in Downloads or Files rather than opening straight into the calendar — tap it there and your phone will offer to add the events. The feeds are a guess at your kitchen, not a law: if the jar is running ahead or behind, move the events. Deleting the series is one long-press on any of them.

For your grain bins

Where this gets adjusted

  • Ancient grains ferment fast and collapse fast. Einkorn, khorasan and emmer have weak, extensible gluten and high enzyme activity. Drop hydration 5–10 points against the table above, cut the bulk short, and watch the dough rather than the timer.
  • Lentil flour dilutes the gluten further. On the Küllüoba loaf — 450 g khorasan and 50 g lentil — the 50 g of Lev’blé becomes roughly 100 g of levain, and this is the recipe where a 1 g pinch of instant yeast alongside the levain is worth it: levain mixte, the French tradition allowance of 0.2% of flour weight, enough to guarantee lift without flattening the sourness.
  • Enriched doughs need the same help. Oil, honey and eggs slow a levain down badly. The artolaganon is a candidate for the same pinch.
  • Altitude changes proofing, not the formula. Above about 4,000 ft doughs rise faster and over-proof sooner. Shorten bulk, shape a little tighter, and consider dropping hydration a few points.
  • A live levain is also the historically honest choice. Lev’blé is deactivated sourdough plus selected baker’s yeast — flavour without the biology. Bronze Age Anatolia had the biology and nothing else.

Percentages are on total flour, levain flour included. Bake loss assumed at 13% for a free-standing hearth loaf; a tin loaf or a longer bake will run 1–3 points higher.
